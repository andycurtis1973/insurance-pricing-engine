Half your shoppers will pay for more coverage.
A quarter will walk if you push. Your quote treats them the same.

Every insurer competes on the cheapest quote. But in real auto-insurance shopping sessions, only a quarter bought the cheapest thing they saw — and nearly half traded up to more coverage. Here's a model that tells them apart, and an engine that learns which is which from clicks.

Two shoppers, one price tag

Each person picked one offer from the menu they were shown. A Bayesian choice model — a conditional logit with a Laplace posterior, so every number has a credible interval — recovers how much price and coverage drove the pick, and how that shifts with who the shopper is. Every coefficient below excludes zero.

What's real here, and what isn't

Before a single dollar of "lift," the honest part. This dataset is buyers-only: everyone in it bought something, so it can't tell us who would have walked away at a higher price. That walk-away risk is exactly what makes differentiated pricing pay off — so to test the engine, we add it, and we label it.

      It learns who wants what — from clicks

      The offline run assumed we already knew each segment's response. You don't — you learn it. This is a Thompson Sampling bandit: context is the segment, the arms are lead-with-lean / mid / rich, the reward is revenue. It explores, then exploits.

      price-driven balanced coverage-driven

      The response it learns is the whole story: push a price-driven shopper to richer coverage and their conversion falls (%→%); push a coverage-driven one and it rises (%→%). Same offer, opposite result.

      bandit (learns from clicks) compete on price oracle (knows the answer)

      It converges to within % of a perfect-knowledge oracle, locking each segment's best offer within a few hundred to a few thousand shoppers — while competing-on-price leaves money on the table the entire time. No elasticities were handed to it; it found them.
